Curriculum purpose

Why are we learning this?

Water is part of every day: cooking, washing, drinking and growing food. Knowing how materials mix and how to clean water helps you, your family and your community.

Science

Understand your world

You will learn how scientists sort, separate, dissolve and clean materials. By the end of the term you will design, build and test a water filter.

Language

Use all your languages

Your home language is a tool for thinking. You will use it to understand ideas first, and then learn to explain them in English the way scientists do.

Skills

Work like a scientist

You will predict, test fairly, record results, solve a real problem as a team and share what you found with your class and your family.

Our big goal

By the end of Unit 3 you will explain to your class and your family, in English and in your home language, how your team’s filter makes water cleaner, and why filtered water must still be boiled before anyone drinks it.

  • Every lesson: quick checks such as thumbs up, a one-question exit ticket and your teacher watching your team work. These help you, not your report card.
  • End of each unit: one “Show what you know” task. You will see the marking guide before you start.

  • You can choose your challenge level: draw and label, fill in a sentence frame, or write in your own words. Each level can reach full science marks.

Learn

1. Everything is made of particles

All materials are made of tiny pieces called particles. They are far too small to see. In a solid the particles are packed tightly and stay in place. In a liquid they are close together but can slide past each other, so a liquid can be poured. In a gas they are far apart and move quickly in all directions.

2. What is a mixture?

A mixture is two or more materials put together. Each material keeps its own properties: in umngqusho, the samp is still samp and the beans are still beans. Mixtures are everywhere: muddy water after rain, sand and stones in the yard, oil and water in a pan.

  • Solid + solid: samp and beans, sand and gravel
  • Solid + liquid: sand and water, mud
  • Liquid + liquid: cooking oil and water (they form layers)

3. Separating a mixture

Because each material keeps its properties, we can often separate it again. We choose a method by looking at the size and kind of the pieces.

Key words

Language

Word detective: melt or dissolve?

In English, melt and dissolve are two different things. Ice melts when it gets warm. Sugar dissolves when it mixes into water. In some languages one word is used for both. Ask your family: is that true in your language? Share what you found with your team.

Learn

1. A solution is a special mixture

Stir sugar into water and the sugar seems to disappear. It is still there: the water tastes sweet. The sugar has dissolved. It has broken up into particles so small that they spread out between the water particles. Now you cannot see the sugar, and the mixture looks the same all the way through. This kind of mixture is called a solution.

The material that dissolves is the solute (sugar). The liquid it dissolves in is the solvent (water). This is why a filter from Unit 1 cannot get sugar out of water: the sugar particles are far smaller than the holes in any cloth or paper.

2. Soluble or insoluble?

A substance that dissolves in water is soluble, like sugar, salt and instant coffee. A substance that does not dissolve is insoluble, like sand, chalk and cooking oil. Insoluble solids make the water cloudy or sink to the bottom.

3. Saturated solutions

Keep adding salt to a cup of water and stirring. At first it all dissolves. Then the grains start staying at the bottom. The water cannot hold any more: it is a saturated solution.

4. Getting the solute back

Leave salty water in a shallow lid in the sun. The water slowly evaporates into the air and the salt is left behind as crystals. This is how sea salt is made.

5. What makes dissolving faster?

  • Warmer water: particles move faster, so the solute breaks up and spreads sooner.
  • Stirring or shaking: brings fresh water to the solute.
  • Smaller grains: fine sugar has more surface touching the water than a sugar cube.

To find out properly, scientists do a fair test: change only one thing, keep everything else the same, and measure the result.

Activity 4 · Fast or slow? A fair test

Teams · 3 lessons
ReaderRecorderMaterials managerReporter · timekeeper

Your team chooses one question:

  • Does sugar dissolve faster in warm water or cold water?
  • Does sugar dissolve faster if we stir it?
  • Does fine sugar dissolve faster than a sugar cube or coarse sugar?
  1. Plan: what will you change? What will you keep the same (amount of water, amount of sugar, cup)? What will you measure (seconds until no sugar can be seen)?
  2. Predict: write your prediction with a reason.
  3. Test: do it three times. Count seconds aloud or use a phone timer.
  4. Record: put your times in a table and draw a bar graph.
  5. Conclude: was your prediction right? Explain using particles.

Key words

Language

Learn

1. How water gets polluted

Water in rivers, dams and streams mixes with many things. When harmful things get into water, we call it pollution. Using what you learnt in Unit 2, we can sort pollutants:

  • Insoluble pollutants you can often see: plastic, cans, glass, oil, tyres and sewage (toilet waste).
  • Soluble pollutants dissolve and are hidden: soap and washing powder, fertiliser and some poisons.

2. Wetlands: nature’s filters

A wetland is land that is soaked with water for much of the year, like a marsh or vlei. Wetlands work like a sponge: they slow water down, plants and soil trap dirt, and some pollution is broken down. Wetlands are also homes for frogs, birds, fish and insects. Protecting wetlands helps keep our water cleaner.

3. How water is made safe

At a water treatment works, water is screened (sieved) to remove rubbish, left to settle so mud sinks, filtered through layers of sand and gravel, and then a chemical such as chlorine is added to kill germs.

At home, settling and filtering make water clearer, but they do not remove dissolved pollution and they do not kill germs. Boiling kills germs.

Our safety rule

Clear water is not always safe water. Never drink water from your filter. Water from a filter must still be boiled, or treated by an adult, before anyone drinks it.

Activity 4 · Design challenge: build a water filter

Teams · 6 lessons · this is your term practical task

The brief: A family collects rainwater in a tank. After a storm the water is cloudy with soil. Design and make a filter that makes the water as clear as possible, using only recycled or very cheap materials, that fits in a 2-litre bottle.

  1. Investigate: test single materials (sand, gravel, cloth, cotton wool, charcoal). Which one makes muddy water clearest?
  2. Design: draw your filter, label every layer and give a reason for the order. Get your teacher to sign the design.
  3. Make: cut the bottom off the bottle, turn it upside down over a cup and build your layers. Rinse the sand and gravel first.
  4. Test: pour one cup of the same muddy water through. Score the water before and after with the clarity card.
  5. Evaluate and improve: what worked? Change one thing, test again and compare scores.
  6. Communicate: make your two-language poster and practise your two-minute talk.

How to use the clarity card

Your teacher gives each team a card with five lines of letters, from big to tiny. Hold it behind a clear cup of water. Count how many lines you can still read through the water. 0 means very cloudy; 5 means very clear. Use the same cup and the same card every time, so your test is fair.

Stay safe

An adult cuts the bottles and crushes the charcoal (only cold, used braai charcoal). Never drink or taste filtered water. Wash your hands after testing.

Running it with 40 to 50 learners
  • Make 10 to 12 teams of four. Mix home languages and English confidence in every team, so each team has a stronger English reader and a strong home-language explainer.
  • Pack one crate or box of materials per station; the Materials manager collects and returns it. Rotate stations so only four teams need the same equipment at once.
  • Give each challenge level for the same task; learners choose, and you nudge. Everyone works on the same science content.
  • Use the observation checklist on two or three teams per lesson, so every team is observed at least once a week.
Using the site with few devices or little data
  • Open the site once on your phone or laptop and project it, or pass the phone around team by team. It is a single light page with no video.
  • Copy the “Do” sections and frames onto the board or onto one paper copy per team (about 12 copies, not 50).
  • The Listen buttons use the device’s built-in voice; on most phones they work without extra data.
  • Learners’ “I can” ticks and home-language words save only on the device used. On a shared phone, use them as a whole-class check.
Language support: how we use home languages
  • Talk first, write second: every unit opens with talk in any language, then moves to English key words and sentence frames.
  • Science and language are marked separately, so a learner who understands the science is not hidden by limited English.
  • Home-language words are collected, not imposed. Learners and families supply them. Please check the class word wall with an isiZulu- or isiXhosa-speaking colleague before it goes up.
  • Code-switching to explain an idea is welcome. Keep English key terms in written products, as formal CAPS tasks are written in the LoLT.
Safety checklist
  • Warm water only, poured by the teacher. No boiling water in class.
  • No tasting during any activity. Filtered water is never drunk.
  • An adult cuts bottles and crushes only cold, used charcoal.
  • Hand washing after every practical.
